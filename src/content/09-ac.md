@phase 9 | AC fundamentals | Frequency, phase, RMS, impedance and resonance

# sine-wave: Sine waves
kind: concept
level: 1
needs: ac-intro
viz: phasor
> The natural shape of oscillation, set by amplitude, frequency and phase.

A rotating generator naturally produces a sine wave. Sines are also special mathematically: pass one through any linear circuit, and you get a sine at the same frequency out. Only the amplitude and phase change.

Why sines? Anything that rotates steadily, or swings like a pendulum, produces one. Also, the derivative of a sine is another sine, so capacitors and inductors keep the shape and only shift and scale it.

## Math
$$v(t) = V_p\sin(\omega t + \phi)$$ General sinusoid.

## Check
Q: A sine wave goes through a linear circuit. What can change?
+ Its amplitude and phase
- Its frequency
- Its shape
W: Linear circuits add no new frequencies.

# frequency: Frequency and period
kind: quantity
level: 1
needs: sine-wave
viz: phasor
> How many cycles occur per second, and how long one cycle takes.

| Signal | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Indian mains | 50 Hz |
| Human hearing | 20 Hz to 20 kHz |
| FM radio | About 100 MHz |
| Wi-Fi | 2.4 and 5 GHz |

## Math
$$f = \frac1T$$ Frequency in hertz.
$$\omega = 2\pi f$$ Angular frequency in rad/s.

## Example
Q: What is the period of 50 Hz mains?
- $T = 1/50$
A: 20 ms.
Q: A crystal runs at 8 MHz. What is its period?
- $T = 1/(8\times10^{6})$
A: 125 ns.

## Check
Q: What is the period of a 1 kHz signal?
+ 1 ms
- 1 s
- 1 µs
W: T = 1/f = 1/1000 s.

# phase: Phase
kind: quantity
level: 2
needs: frequency, trigonometry
viz: phasor
> How far one wave is shifted in time relative to another.

Two sines at the same frequency may peak at different times. Their shift, measured in degrees or radians, is the phase difference. A capacitor's current **leads** its voltage by 90°; an inductor's current **lags** by 90°.

The memory aid is **ELI the ICE man**: voltage (E) leads current (I) in an inductor (L); current leads voltage in a capacitor (C).

## Math
$$\phi = 360°\times\frac{\Delta t}{T}$$ Phase from time delay.

## Example
Q: Two 50 Hz waves are offset by 1 ms. What is the phase difference?
- $T = 20$ ms
- $360\times1/20$
A: 18°.

## Check
Q: In a capacitor, how does current relate to voltage?
+ Current leads by 90°
- Current lags by 90°
- They are in phase
W: Current must flow first to build up the voltage.

# rms: RMS value
kind: quantity
level: 1
needs: sine-wave, power-formulas
viz: phasor
> The DC value that would deliver the same heating power.

An AC wave's average is zero, so average tells you nothing about power. Instead: square it, average, take the root. That gives the equivalent DC heating value.

RMS depends on wave shape. Square wave: equal to peak. Triangle: peak over $\sqrt3$. PWM between 0 and $V$: $V\sqrt{D}$.

## Math
$$V_{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^Tv^2\,dt}$$ General definition.
$$V_{RMS} = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt2}$$ Sine waves only.

## Example
Q: Indian mains is 230 V RMS. What is the peak?
- $V_p = 230\times\sqrt2$
A: 325 V. Components must survive the peak, not the RMS value.

## Watch out
A cheap meter assumes a sine wave and scales its reading. On a PWM signal it can be badly wrong, so use a true RMS meter.

## Check
Q: A sine wave has a 10 V peak. What is its RMS value?
+ 7.07 V
- 10 V
- 14.1 V
W: 10/√2 = 7.07 V.

# phasor: Phasors
kind: method
level: 2
needs: complex-numbers, phase
viz: phasor
> A rotating arrow that stands in for a sine wave.

Since every signal in a linear AC circuit has the same frequency, only amplitude and phase need tracking. A phasor stores just those, as a complex number. Differentiation becomes multiplication by $j\omega$, so calculus turns into arithmetic.

## Math
$$V_p\sin(\omega t+\phi)\ \leftrightarrow\ V_p\angle\phi$$ Sine to phasor.
$$\frac{d}{dt}\ \leftrightarrow\ j\omega$$ Derivative becomes a multiplication.

## Example
Q: Add $3\angle0°$ and $4\angle90°$.
- In rectangular form: $3+j4$
A: $5\angle53.1°$.

## Check
Q: Why can phasors ignore frequency?
+ All signals in the circuit share one frequency
- Frequency has no effect on AC
- They only work at 50 Hz
W: Only amplitude and phase differ, so only they need tracking.

# reactance: Reactance
kind: quantity
level: 2
needs: phasor, capacitor, inductor
viz: rlc
> Frequency-dependent opposition from capacitors and inductors.

Reactance is like resistance, but stores energy instead of burning it. Capacitors pass high frequency, so reactance falls as $f$ rises. Inductors block it, so reactance rises.

## Math
$$X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi fC}$$ Capacitive reactance.
$$X_L = 2\pi fL$$ Inductive reactance.

## Example
Q: Find $X_C$ for 100 nF at 1 MHz.
- $X_C = 1/(2\pi\times10^6\times10^{-7})$
A: 1.59 Ω, close to a short for noise. That is why it is used for decoupling.
Q: Find $X_L$ for 10 µH at 1 MHz.
- $2\pi\times10^6\times10^{-5}$
A: 62.8 Ω.

## Check
Q: Frequency doubles. What happens to capacitive reactance?
+ It halves
- It doubles
- It stays the same
W: X_C = 1/(2πfC).

# impedance: Impedance
kind: quantity
level: 2
needs: reactance
viz: rlc
> Total AC opposition: resistance and reactance as one complex number.

Impedance $Z$ is Ohm's law generalised to AC. With it, every DC method (series, parallel, nodal, Thevenin) works unchanged.

## Math
$$Z = R + jX$$ Complex impedance.
$$|Z| = \sqrt{R^2+X^2}$$ Magnitude.
$$\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{I}Z$$ Ohm's law for AC.
$$Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C},\quad Z_L = j\omega L$$ Component impedances.

## Example
Q: Find $|Z|$ for $R = 3\ \Omega$ with $X_L = 4\ \Omega$.
- $|Z| = \sqrt{9+16}$
A: 5 Ω, with phase 53.1°.
Q: 1 kΩ in series with 1 µF at 159 Hz. Find $|Z|$.
- $X_C = 1/(2\pi\times159\times10^{-6}) \approx 1$ kΩ
- $|Z| = \sqrt{1000^2+1000^2}$
A: About 1.41 kΩ, at −45°.

## Check
Q: Resistance is 6 Ω and reactance is 8 Ω. What is |Z|?
+ 10 Ω
- 14 Ω
- 2 Ω
W: √(36 + 64) = 10. They add at right angles.

# resonance: Resonance
kind: concept
level: 2
needs: impedance
viz: rlc
> At one frequency L and C cancel, and energy swings between them.

When $X_L = X_C$, the reactances cancel. In a series RLC the impedance falls to just $R$; current peaks. Energy swaps between the capacitor's field and the inductor's field, like a child on a swing.

## Math
$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$ Resonant frequency.
$$Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$$ Quality factor for series RLC.
$$BW = \frac{f_0}{Q}$$ Bandwidth.

## Example
Q: Find $f_0$ for 10 µH and 100 pF.
- $\sqrt{LC} = \sqrt{10^{-15}} = 3.16\times10^{-8}$
A: About 5.03 MHz.
Q: Find Q for 10 µH, 100 pF and 10 Ω in series.
- $\sqrt{L/C} = 316\ \Omega$
- $Q = 316/10$
A: About 32.

## Watch out
Unwanted resonance appears everywhere: between a cable and a capacitor, or in a filter with low damping. It causes voltage peaks far above the supply.

## EgireRobotics
A battery lead's inductance and an ESC's capacitors form a resonant circuit. That's why low-ESR capacitors sometimes need a damping resistor or some ESR.

## Check
Q: At series resonance, the impedance is:
+ Minimum, equal to R
- Maximum
- Zero always
W: X_L and X_C cancel, leaving resistance.

# bode: Filters and Bode plots
kind: method
level: 2
needs: impedance, logarithms-db
see: laplace, active-filter
viz: filter
> Plots of gain and phase against frequency.

A filter passes some frequencies and blocks others. A simple RC low-pass: at low frequency, the capacitor is open and the signal passes; at high frequency it shorts the signal to ground.

The corner frequency is where output is down 3 dB. Beyond it, a first-order filter falls by 20 dB per decade.

## Math
$$f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$$ Corner frequency.
$$H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1+j\omega RC}$$ Low-pass transfer function.
$$|H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}}$$ Magnitude.

## Example
Q: Design a 1 kHz low-pass with $C = 100$ nF.
- $R = 1/(2\pi\times1000\times10^{-7})$
A: 1.59 kΩ.

## Lab
Sweep an RC filter with a signal generator. Record output at each frequency, then plot gain in dB on a log frequency axis.

## EgireRobotics
Gyro data is filtered with low-pass and notch filters to cut motor vibration noise. Filter delay reduces flight stability, so it is a trade-off.

## Check
Q: How fast does a first-order filter fall above its corner?
+ 20 dB per decade
- 3 dB per decade
- 60 dB per decade
W: Each tenfold frequency rise cuts amplitude tenfold.

# ac-power: AC power and power factor
kind: quantity
level: 2
needs: rms, impedance
> Real, reactive and apparent power form a triangle.

If voltage and current are out of phase, part of the energy just sloshes back and forth.

## Math
$$P = V_{RMS}I_{RMS}\cos\phi$$ Real power (W).
$$Q = V_{RMS}I_{RMS}\sin\phi$$ Reactive power (VAR).
$$S = V_{RMS}I_{RMS}$$ Apparent power (VA).

## Example
Q: 230 V, 5 A, power factor 0.8. Find the real power.
- $P = 230\times5\times0.8$
A: 920 W.

## Check
Q: Why do utilities care about power factor?
+ Low power factor means more current for the same real power
- It changes the frequency
- It makes voltage rise
W: Extra current heats lines while delivering no useful power.

# three-phase: Three-phase power
kind: concept
level: 2
needs: phase, ac-power
see: bldc
viz: esc
> Three sines spaced 120° apart deliver smooth, constant power.

Three windings 120° apart give constant total power and a rotating magnetic field. Grids, industrial motors and every brushless drone motor use it.

## Engineering
A rotating field comes for free. Three coils spaced 120° apart, fed by three phases, produce a field that spins smoothly. This is why induction and brushless motors don't need a commutator.

## Math
$$V_L = \sqrt3V_{ph}$$ Line voltage in a star connection.
$$P = \sqrt3V_LI_L\cos\phi$$ Total three-phase power.

## Example
Q: Indian phase voltage is 230 V. What is line voltage?
- $V_L = 1.732\times230$
A: About 400 V.

# harmonics: Harmonics and spectra
kind: concept
level: 3
needs: fourier, frequency
see: emi
viz: fourier
> Non-sine waveforms contain energy at multiples of the base frequency.

A square wave at 1 MHz has energy at 3, 5, 7 MHz and so on. Faster edges raise the amount of high-frequency energy, so a slow clock with sharp edges can still radiate at hundreds of MHz.

## Math
$$f_{\text{knee}} \approx \frac{0.35}{t_r}$$ Bandwidth of an edge with rise time $t_r$.

## Example
Q: A 1 ns edge contains energy up to about what frequency?
- $0.35/(10^{-9})$
A: About 350 MHz.

## Check
Q: Which harmonics does an ideal square wave contain?
+ Only odd harmonics
- Only even harmonics
- Only the fundamental
W: Its symmetry cancels even terms.

# transmission-line: Transmission lines
kind: concept
level: 3
needs: impedance, em-waves
see: signal-integrity
> When wires are long compared with an edge, they become waveguides.

Signals travel at about 15 cm/ns on FR-4. When the trace delay nears the rise time, the far end does not instantly see the near end's voltage. A line has a characteristic impedance, and mismatches make reflections.

## Math
$$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$ Characteristic impedance.
$$\Gamma = \frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}$$ Reflection coefficient.

## Example
Q: A 50 Ω line ends in 150 Ω. What fraction of the voltage reflects?
- $\Gamma = 100/200$
A: 0.5, half the incident voltage.

## Check
Q: What happens when a line's end matches its $Z_0$?
+ No reflection occurs
- All energy reflects
- The signal doubles
W: Γ = 0 when Z_L = Z₀.

# notch-filter: Notch filters
kind: method
level: 3
needs: bode, resonance
see: imu-sensors
> Removes one narrow band of frequencies and passes the rest.

A notch filter cuts a narrow band around one frequency, with little delay elsewhere. It's a cheap way of removing a known interference. A digital version is one line of code in a flight controller.

## Math
$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$$ Twin-T notch frequency.
$$Q = \frac{f_0}{BW}$$ Narrowness of the notch.

## Example
Q: A motor spins at 12,000 rpm. Where should a gyro notch go?
- $12000/60$
A: At 200 Hz, and it should follow motor speed.

## EgireRobotics
RPM filtering uses ESC telemetry to move notches with each motor's speed, which removes noise while adding little delay.
